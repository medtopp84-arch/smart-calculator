Concrete Volume Calculator: Slab, Footing Volume and Weight

This calculator finds how much concrete a rectangular element needs and how much it weighs. Enter the length, width and thickness, the number of identical elements and an allowance for waste. It returns the net volume, the volume to order, and the weight in tonnes. It suits slabs, floors, strip and pad footings, foundation beds, paths and other rectangular pours.

How the calculation works

A rectangular element is a prism, so its volume is length times width times thickness. The weight is the volume multiplied by the density of the concrete. Because some concrete is always lost or overused, the amount ordered is usually a little larger than the theoretical volume.

Net volume V = L × W × D × Quantity
Volume to order = V × (1 + Waste % ÷ 100)
Weight (tonnes) = V × Density (kg/m³) ÷ 1000

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter the length, width and thickness in meters.
  2. Enter how many identical elements you are casting (for example 4 footings).
  3. Choose an allowance for waste, usually between 5 % and 10 %.
  4. Select the concrete density.
  5. Read the net volume, the volume to order and the weight.

The weight is calculated from the net volume, because the extra ordered concrete is not part of the finished element.

Worked example 1: floor slab

A slab is 10 m long, 5 m wide and 0.2 m thick. One slab, 5 % waste, reinforced concrete at 2400 kg/m³.

  • Net volume: 10 × 5 × 0.2 = 10 m³
  • Volume to order: 10 × 1.05 = 10.5 m³
  • Weight: 10 × 2400 ÷ 1000 = 24 tonnes

Worked example 2: four pad footings

Four identical footings, each 1.5 m × 1.5 m × 0.4 m, with 10 % waste and 2400 kg/m³.

  • Volume of one footing: 1.5 × 1.5 × 0.4 = 0.9 m³
  • Net volume for four: 0.9 × 4 = 3.6 m³
  • Volume to order: 3.6 × 1.10 = 3.96 m³
  • Weight: 3.6 × 2400 ÷ 1000 = 8.64 tonnes

Worked example 3: garage floor

A floor 6 m by 4 m and 0.1 m thick, with 5 % waste and 2400 kg/m³.

  • Net volume: 6 × 4 × 0.1 = 2.4 m³
  • Volume to order: 2.4 × 1.05 = 2.52 m³
  • Weight: 2.4 × 2400 ÷ 1000 = 5.76 tonnes

Concrete density

The unit weight depends on the aggregate, the mix and the amount of reinforcement. The options in the calculator are commonly used planning values. If you have a mix design or a supplier data sheet, use the density given there.

Allowing for waste

Ordering exactly the calculated volume often leaves you short. Reasons include uneven sub-base or excavation, formwork that bulges under load, spillage, concrete left in the pump or the truck, and measuring tolerances. An allowance of about 5 % to 10 % is common practice, but the right figure depends on the site, the type of element and the supplier, so treat it as a starting point.

What this calculator does not cover

  • Non-rectangular shapes. For circular columns, sloped elements or stepped footings, calculate each part separately and add them. A round column has volume π × (D ÷ 2)² × height.
  • Openings and voids. Subtract the volume of openings, pipes or embedded voids if they are significant.
  • Mix quantities. Cement, sand, aggregate and water depend on the mix design and are not calculated here.
  • Structural design. The result is a quantity and a dead-load estimate, not a design of the element.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the thickness in centimeters. A 20 cm slab must be entered as 0.2 m.
  • Forgetting to multiply by the number of elements. Use the quantity field.
  • Using the ordered volume for the weight. The weight of the structure follows the net volume.
  • Ignoring the sub-base level. If the ground is lower than planned, the real thickness will be larger than the design thickness.

Frequently asked questions

How many cubic meters of concrete do I need for a slab?

Multiply length by width by thickness, all in meters, then add your waste allowance. A 4 m × 3 m slab that is 0.15 m thick needs 1.8 m³ net.

How much does a cubic meter of concrete weigh?

About 2300 to 2500 kg for normal-weight concrete, so roughly 2.3 to 2.5 tonnes. The calculator uses 2400 kg/m³ by default.

How do I convert cubic meters to cubic yards?

Multiply by 1.308. For example, 10 m³ is about 13.08 cubic yards.

Is the weight the same as the load on the ground?

Not exactly. It is the self-weight of the concrete. Design loads also include the live load, finishes and other elements. See our disclaimer about using results for final design.
