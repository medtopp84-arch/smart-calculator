Flow Rate Calculator: Area × Velocity to m³/s, L/s and m³/h

This calculator converts the size of a flow path and the speed of the fluid into a volumetric flow rate. You can enter the cross-sectional area directly or enter a pipe inner diameter and let the calculator find the area. Results are given in cubic meters per second, liters per second, cubic meters per hour and liters per hour. It is useful for sizing pumps and pipes, checking water supply and drainage lines, and converting between flow units.

The continuity equation

For an incompressible fluid that fills the flow path, the volume passing a point each second equals the cross-sectional area times the average velocity. This is the basic form of the continuity equation.

Q = A × V
Circular pipe: A = π × (D ÷ 2)²
Q (L/s) = Q (m³/s) × 1000  |  Q (m³/h) = Q (m³/s) × 3600

Here Q is the flow rate in m³/s, A the cross-sectional area in m² and V the average velocity in m/s. Rearranging gives the velocity when you know the flow: V = Q ÷ A.

How to use the calculator

  1. Choose whether you know the area or the pipe inner diameter.
  2. Enter the area in m², or the inner diameter in millimeters.
  3. Enter the average fluid velocity in m/s.
  4. Read the flow rate in the unit you need.

Worked example 1: known area

A channel section has a flow area of 0.2 m² and the water moves at 2.5 m/s.

  • Q = 0.2 × 2.5 = 0.5 m³/s
  • In liters per second: 0.5 × 1000 = 500 L/s
  • In cubic meters per hour: 0.5 × 3600 = 1800 m³/h
  • In liters per hour: 1,800,000 L/h

Worked example 2: known pipe diameter

Water flows at 1.5 m/s in a pipe with an inner diameter of 200 mm.

  • Area: π × 0.1² = 0.031416 m²
  • Q = 0.031416 × 1.5 = 0.04712 m³/s
  • In liters per second: 47.12 L/s
  • In cubic meters per hour: 169.65 m³/h

Worked example 3: finding the velocity

A pump delivers 100 m³/h into a pipe with an inner diameter of 150 mm. What is the velocity?

  • Q = 100 ÷ 3600 = 0.027778 m³/s
  • Area: π × 0.075² = 0.017671 m²
  • V = 0.027778 ÷ 0.017671 = 1.57 m/s

To do this with the calculator, try velocities until the flow matches, or use the formula V = Q ÷ A directly.

Choosing a design velocity

Velocity that is too low lets sediment settle, and velocity that is too high causes friction losses, noise, erosion and surge problems. Suitable values depend on the fluid, the pipe material, the application (suction, discharge, gravity drain, fire main) and the local standard. Many water supply lines are designed for velocities of roughly 1 to 2 m/s, but always check your project specification or the applicable code instead of relying on a general figure.

Assumptions and limits

  • Full flow. The formula assumes the fluid fills the whole cross-section. For a partly full pipe or an open channel, the area depends on the water depth and must be computed first.
  • Average velocity. The velocity is the mean across the section. Velocity is lower near the walls and higher at the center.
  • Incompressible fluid. This is accurate for liquids. For gases, the density changes with pressure, so volumetric flow varies along the line.
  • No pressure loss. This is only the flow relationship. Head loss, pump curves and Reynolds number are separate calculations.

Common mistakes

  • Using outside diameter. Use the inner diameter of the pipe.
  • Mixing millimeters and meters. In diameter mode the calculator expects millimeters, and in area mode square meters.
  • Mixing hours and seconds. The velocity is per second, and the hourly values are the flow multiplied by 3600.
  • Forgetting that area grows with the square of the diameter. Doubling the diameter increases the flow four times at the same velocity.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate flow rate from pipe diameter and velocity?

Find the area with π × (D ÷ 2)² using D in meters, then multiply by the velocity. For 200 mm and 1.5 m/s, that gives 0.0471 m³/s.

How do I convert m³/s to liters per minute?

Multiply by 1000 to get liters per second, then by 60. For example, 0.0471 m³/s is 47.1 L/s, or about 2827 L/min.

What if the pipe changes diameter?

The flow stays the same for an incompressible fluid, so A₁ × V₁ = A₂ × V₂. The velocity rises where the pipe narrows and drops where it widens.

Is this the same as the pump flow rate?

It is the flow the pipe carries at that velocity. The actual flow of a pump depends on the pump curve and the system resistance. See our disclaimer about using results for final design.
