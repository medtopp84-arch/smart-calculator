Dilution Calculator: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ for Stock Solutions

This calculator solves the dilution equation C₁V₁ = C₂V₂. Enter any three of the four values and it finds the missing one. It also gives the dilution factor and the volume of solvent you need to add. It is useful for preparing working solutions from stock solutions in chemistry, biology and clinical labs, for making reagents from concentrated acids, and for planning serial dilutions.

What is dilution?

Dilution lowers the concentration of a solute by adding more solvent. The amount of solute does not change, only the volume that contains it. Because the amount of solute is concentration times volume, the amount before dilution (C₁ × V₁) equals the amount after dilution (C₂ × V₂).

C₁ × V₁ = C₂ × V₂
V₁ = C₂ × V₂ ÷ C₁  |  C₂ = C₁ × V₁ ÷ V₂
Dilution factor DF = C₁ ÷ C₂ = V₂ ÷ V₁
Solvent to add = V₂ − V₁

C₁ is the stock concentration, V₁ the volume of stock you take, C₂ the final concentration and V₂ the final total volume.

How to use the calculator

  1. Pick the units for concentration and volume. They are used for labels only and the math is the same for any unit.
  2. Fill in three of the four boxes and leave one empty.
  3. The empty value is calculated automatically, together with the dilution factor and the solvent volume.
  4. To solve for a different unknown, clear that box and fill the one you no longer need.

Important: C₁ and C₂ must use the same concentration unit, and V₁ and V₂ must use the same volume unit.

Worked example 1: volume of stock needed

You have a 10 M stock and need 500 mL of a 1 M solution.

  • V₁ = C₂ × V₂ ÷ C₁ = 1 × 500 ÷ 10 = 50 mL of stock
  • Solvent to add: 500 − 50 = 450 mL
  • Dilution factor: 10 ÷ 1 = 10

In practice, put the stock in a volumetric flask and add solvent up to the 500 mL mark, instead of adding exactly 450 mL.

Worked example 2: mass concentration

A 5 mg/mL stock is used to make 20 mL of a 0.5 mg/mL solution.

  • V₁ = 0.5 × 20 ÷ 5 = 2 mL of stock
  • Solvent to add: 20 − 2 = 18 mL

Worked example 3: final concentration

25 mL of a 2 M solution is diluted to a final volume of 100 mL.

  • C₂ = C₁ × V₁ ÷ V₂ = 2 × 25 ÷ 100 = 0.5 M
  • Dilution factor: 100 ÷ 25 = 4

Worked example 4: final volume

10 mL of a 6 M solution should be diluted to 0.5 M. What final volume is needed?

  • V₂ = C₁ × V₁ ÷ C₂ = 6 × 10 ÷ 0.5 = 120 mL
  • Solvent to add: 120 − 10 = 110 mL

Serial dilutions

When you need a very large dilution, it is more accurate to do it in steps. In a serial dilution, each tube is diluted from the previous one by the same factor. The total dilution is the factor raised to the number of steps.

  • 1:10 steps, taking 1 mL into 9 mL of solvent each time, give a factor of 10 per step.
  • After 3 steps the total factor is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000.
  • A 1 M stock becomes 0.1 M, then 0.01 M, then 0.001 M (1 mM).

Be careful with wording. A "1:10 dilution" usually means 1 part stock in 10 parts total (factor 10), but some people mean 1 part stock plus 10 parts solvent (factor 11). State the factor and the volumes to avoid mistakes.

Assumptions and limits

  • The solute amount stays the same. The equation does not hold if solute is added, lost, or consumed by a reaction, or if the solvent evaporates.
  • Volumes are treated as additive. This is a good approximation for dilute aqueous solutions. For concentrated acids, alcohol and water mixtures and some other systems, the mixed volume is slightly different from the sum, so dilute in a volumetric flask up to the mark.
  • Concentration changes with temperature. Molarity depends on volume, and volume depends on temperature. Prepare and use standards at the specified temperature.
  • Only a decrease in concentration. C₂ must be smaller than C₁, and V₂ larger than V₁. Otherwise you would be concentrating the solution, not diluting it.

Safety when diluting concentrated solutions

Diluting strong acids and bases releases heat. The standard practice is to add the acid or base slowly to the water, never the water to the acid, and to work with proper protective equipment and ventilation. Follow your laboratory procedures and the safety data sheet of each chemical.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing units. Using mM for C₁ and M for C₂, or mL and L for the volumes, gives wrong answers.
  • Treating V₂ as the solvent volume. V₂ is the total final volume. The solvent is V₂ − V₁.
  • Adding V₂ of solvent to V₁ of stock. That gives a total volume of V₁ + V₂, not V₂.
  • Forgetting purity or density. When the stock is given as a percentage by mass, convert it to a concentration first, for example with the Mass Percent Calculator and the Molarity Calculator.

Frequently asked questions

What is the dilution factor?

It is the ratio of the stock concentration to the final concentration, C₁ ÷ C₂, which equals V₂ ÷ V₁. A factor of 10 means the solution is ten times weaker.

Can I use it with percent or ppm?

Yes. Any concentration unit works as long as C₁ and C₂ use the same one.

How do I make 1 L of 0.1 M HCl from 12 M HCl?

V₁ = 0.1 × 1000 ÷ 12 = 8.33 mL of the 12 M stock, added to water in a 1 L volumetric flask and made up to the mark. Follow the safety practice above.

Does C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ work for mixing two solutions?

Not directly. To mix two solutions of different concentrations, add the amounts of solute in each and divide by the total volume.
